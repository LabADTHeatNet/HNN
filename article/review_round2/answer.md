Comment 2: I would like to express my gratitude to the authors for including Figure 6, as it serves to enhance the clarity of the presentation of the results. I agree with the authors' reasoning regarding the supply-side error caused by the drop in temperature due to sections 41, 32 and 36 being far from the heating plant. However, this would not explain why the model predicts sections 37 and 38 well. Graphically, it appears that section 36 is at 19 hops, whereas sections 37 and 38 are at approximately 17 hops. While I noticed that some explanations follow Figure 8, Figures 5 and 6 require clearer explanation, as they risk making the model appear weak. Alternatively, I suggest that the authors conduct further analyses to see if they can improve the model further.

Response 2: We thank the Reviewer for this important observation. We agree that distance from the heat source alone is not sufficient to explain the observed errors. In the revised manuscript, we expanded the Results and Discussion section to clarify how Figures 5--8 should be interpreted. We now explain that Figure 5 shows only the topological-distance structure of errors, while the specific class pairs should be analyzed using the class-distribution plots and normalized confusion matrices. The revised text discusses segment 41 as a local observability issue caused by the combination of signal attenuation, sparse sensor placement, and unequal section-level representation after pipe grouping; it also explains why other remote segments, such as 37 and 38, can remain distinguishable. We further added a brief discussion of other local ambiguities in both subnets and clarified that these patterns indicate limitations of the current sparse monitoring layout rather than a general weakness of the model. (lines 426-434, lines 453-528, Figures 5-8)

Comment 3: Regarding the new Figures 7 and 8, I thank the authors for the added comment and for including the normalized confusion matrix. However, in these figures, rather than "Predicted wrong," the label in the legend should be called "False positives assigned to this section."

Response 3: We revised the legends in Figures 7 and 8 and the corresponding explanatory text in the manuscript. The previous labels "True", "Predicted Right", and "Predicted Wrong" have been replaced with "Ground Truth", "True Positives", and "False Positives", respectively, so that the terminology is clearer and consistent with the interpretation of the class-distribution panels. Comment 4: The conclusions do not address the observed limitations. While it is true that many errors occur within a limited topological distance, errors on the order of hundreds of metres often occur too. I suggest briefly mentioning the analysis related to the distance of the errors. Furthermore, the issue of accuracy could be emphasised by discussing how this information could be used to determine where to place additional monitoring sensors in a DHN.

Response 4: We agree with the Reviewer. We revised the Results and Discussion section and the Conclusions to explicitly acknowledge that, although most errors are local in topological distance, the metric-distance analysis still reveals larger physical-distance errors in poorly observed regions. We also added a discussion of how these persistent confusion patterns can be used for sensor-layout assessment. In particular, we clarified that the current sparse layout retains pressure and temperature measurements only at the heat-source supply/return boundary nodes and at consumer connection nodes. Therefore, the recurring ambiguity between sections 32, 36, and 41 suggests a local observability limitation and identifies the incident pipe-junction nodes, especially branching junctions, as natural candidate locations for additional measurements. We present this as a direction for future sensor-placement studies, consistent with the optimal sensor-placement framework discussed by Rosich et al., rather than as a definitive claim that one additional sensor would eliminate all errors. (lines 506-528, lines 568-592)
